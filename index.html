The approach

Dementia-safe by design

Every part of a visit is shaped around how dementia affects memory, movement and communication — without ever making a gentleman feel fragile or handled.

Calm, and never rushed

Steady pace, controlled movements, clear speech and a predictable set-up. Nothing sudden, nothing startling.

No live blade. Ever.

The hot towel shave uses a foil shaver, warm lather and a razor with the blade removed. The same routine, the same close finish, none of the risk.

Adapted to the day

Anxious, non-verbal, resistant to touch or bed-bound — I work with the man in front of me, and if today isn’t the day, I come back later.

Left as I found it

Strict infection control, fresh gown and neck strip for every gentleman, and the room cleaned down and handed back exactly as it was.

What makes it different

Most barbers bring clippers. I bring 1962.

A mobile barber turns up, cuts the hair and leaves. I set out to recreate a proper barbershop — because recognition is the thing that brings someone back into the room.

The full hot towel service

Real towels, warm lather, a hot towel cabinet and the whole traditional routine. Almost no mobile barber brings this into a care home. It is the part gentlemen remember.

A television that remembers

An iPad built into a vintage television case, playing the adverts, game show jingles and music videos they grew up with. It starts more conversations than anything else I own.

A wireless in the corner

A vintage radio for background sound that feels like home rather than a clinic. Familiar noise settles a room faster than silence does.

Photographs that are his

A digital frame for the home with a folder for each gentleman, filled with pictures his family have sent. Wedding days, army photographs, holidays. Men tell stories they haven’t told in years.

Why this matters

“I’m here five days a week and he doesn’t remember me.” — A care home manager, after a visit

I called in at one of the homes recently, informally, just to say hello. I asked if I could go and see one of the gentlemen. He was sitting in his wheelchair in silence with tears in his eyes. I went over and he pointed straight at me and said, “You’re the barber!”

I asked him what was wrong. He said his memory was going and he keeps forgetting things — and then immediately asked me how my boy was getting on.

That is the whole reason for this. The hardest part of the work is building real bonds with men who may not remember you the moment you leave the chair. And then one moment like that makes every bit of it worth doing.
